🧶 Folklore

⚠️ These are rumors, not facts.

Everything on this page is what people are saying, summarized by a language model, not what is true. A claim appearing here does not mean it's true, and many are false. Its genre, motif and cast (villain, victim, hero) describe the shape of the story as its tellers tell it, the way a folklorist classifies a legend: they are not truth claims, and they're the same whether people tell the story or argue against it. Where a fact-checker has examined a claim, the check is linked (🔎).

🚧 Work in progress. An experiment in finding the stories people retell in their own words: rumors, legends, beliefs, jokes and sayings, not the news itself. Read from a sample of public Bluesky and Mastodon posts; the labels are a small local language model's best guess and are often wrong.

How this works

Folklore travels as variants: one claim or story told by many unrelated people, each in their own words. Copypasta and coordinated posting travel as the same words. So posts saying the same thing in different words are grouped together (by meaning, with an embedding model), and each group is measured by how many different people posted it and how varied their wording is. Feeds, bots and accounts posting many times an hour are left out.

For each big group, our local language model reads a few versions and says what they share, whether it's retold or just a shared topic, its genre, the recurring motif it's an instance of (in our own motif index), and who it casts as villain, victim and hero. When the posts share articles from outlets we follow, those articles are shown (an exact link to the story); otherwise a link to the day's news appears only when the model, choosing among the closest stories with "none" on offer, picks one. When a fact-checker (PolitiFact, Snopes, FactCheck.org, Lead Stories, Full Fact, NewsGuard, Science Feedback) has examined the same claim in the last 30 days, the check is linked (🔎), chosen the same way.

People's posts are never shown here, only the model's summary of what they share; accounts are never identified. Raw posts are kept 30 days for this research and never published.

🧩 Motif

The recurring shape a story is an instance of, from our own motif index: a list we build as we go, the way Stith Thompson built his Motif-Index of Folk-Literature from the folktales he read, but from today's political talk. Thompson's index (built from folktales, myths and legends) fits modern political rumor poorly, so we don't use it. Each motif is named as a reusable framing, without names or dates, so the same story told about other people or another year lands in the same entry; the model files each claim, and a person refines the entries by hand.

Rumor, conspiracy and subject

  • Subject: a broad family (crime and danger, health, celebrities, government…), our own list, after the way urban-legend collections such as Jan Harold Brunvand's sort legends into chapters.

The story's cast

Vladimir Propp's Morphology of the Folktale (1928) found that folktales cast their characters in a few fixed parts, whatever the plot. Three of them fit a great deal of political storytelling: 🦹 the villain who does harm, 🫂 the victim who suffers it, and 🦸 the hero who fights back or sets things right. The names in each part are the model's words for who the posts cast there, and a part is left out when the posts don't fill it.

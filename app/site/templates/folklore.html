How this works

Folklore travels as variants: one claim or story told by many unrelated people, each in their own words. Copypasta and coordinated posting travel as the same words. So posts saying the same thing in different words are grouped together (by meaning, with an embedding model), and each group is measured by how many different people posted it and how varied their wording is. Feeds, bots and accounts posting many times an hour are left out.

For each big group, our local language model reads a few versions and says what they share, whether it's retold or just a shared topic, its genre, its chapter of Thompson's Motif-Index (the folklorist's catalog of story elements), and who it casts as villain, victim and hero. When the posts share articles from outlets we follow, those articles are shown (an exact link to the story); otherwise a link to the day's news appears only when the model, choosing among the closest stories with "none" on offer, picks one. When a fact-checker (PolitiFact, Snopes, FactCheck.org, Lead Stories, Full Fact, NewsGuard, Science Feedback) has examined the same claim in the last 30 days, the check is linked (🔎), chosen the same way.

People's posts are never shown here, only the model's summary of what they share; accounts are never identified. Raw posts are kept 30 days for this research and never published.

📚 Motif-Index

Stith Thompson's Motif-Index of Folk-Literature (1932–36, revised 1955–58) is the folklorists' standard catalog of the recurring elements in folktales, myths and legends from around the world, sorted into lettered chapters, A to Z. The model picks the chapter a narrative fits best. It's a rough way to see which old story shapes today's narratives reuse. Only chapters are shown: matching a narrative to one of the index's 46,000 specific motifs is still being tested, by hand, and isn't reliable enough to publish. Chapters on this page:

The story's cast

Vladimir Propp's Morphology of the Folktale (1928) found that folktales cast their characters in a few fixed parts, whatever the plot. Three of them fit a great deal of political storytelling: 🦹 the villain who does harm, 🫂 the victim who suffers it, and 🦸 the hero who fights back or sets things right. The names in each part are the model's words for who the posts cast there, and a part is left out when the posts don't fill it.

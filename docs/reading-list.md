# Reading list: the folkloric shape of political discourse

Working thesis: folklore shapes political discourse, and the first step to understanding information warfare is
understanding its folkloric shape.

Books and a few essential articles, on six shelves; (owned) marks the ones on the shelf at home. Each entry says what the work gives the BND Motif Index and the
site. Dates are first publication (for translated works, the original year first). Annotations are summaries in our
own words. Research roadmap: issue #146; the folklore lens: #145.

## Start here, in order

1. **Brunvand, *The Vanishing Hitchhiker*** (owned) — the basic toolkit: variants, the "friend of a friend",
   localizing, and reading a legend for what it worries about. Short and readable.
2. **Frank, *Newslore*** (owned) — Brunvand's ideas applied to the folklore that springs up around news events.
3. **Fine & Ellis, *The Global Grapevine*** — the thesis's closest ancestor: rumors about terrorism and immigration as
   political folklore.
4. **Hochschild, *Strangers in Their Own Land*** — the "deep story": the felt narrative under the facts, which is what
   a motif tries to name.
5. **Dégh, *Legend and Belief*** — why legends are arguments about what's possible, not just stories.
6. **Dundes (ed.), *The Blood Libel Legend*** — one shape followed across eight centuries, down to its modern reuse.
7. **Barkun, *A Culture of Conspiracy*** — the event / systemic / superconspiracy scopes the site already labels with.
8. **Rid, *Active Measures*** — how state disinformation has always worked by riding stories people already tell.
9. **Tangherlini et al., "An automated pipeline…" (2020)** — the computational version of all of the above, closest
   to what BND builds.

## A. Rumor and contemporary legend

What rumors and legends are, why people pass them on, and how they carry a group's fears and grievances.

- [ ] **Robert H. Knapp, "A Psychology of Rumor"** (1944, article, *Public Opinion Quarterly*). Wartime rumors sorted
  by the feeling they serve: wish ("pipe-dream"), dread ("bogie") and wedge-driving.
  *BND:* the source of the wish / dread / wedge rumor chips on Folklore and Rumors.
- [ ] **Gordon W. Allport & Leo Postman, *The Psychology of Rumor*** (1947). The classic lab study of how a story
  levels, sharpens and assimilates as it is retold.
  *BND:* names the drift between a narrative's versions across days (the 🧵 threads).
- [ ] **Tamotsu Shibutani, *Improvised News: A Sociological Study of Rumor*** (1966). Rumor as a group's collective
  problem-solving when official news falls short.
  *BND:* why rumors bloom in the gaps the front pages leave (compare the radio and front-page running orders).
- [ ] **Jan Harold Brunvand, *The Vanishing Hitchhiker: American Urban Legends and Their Meanings*** (1981). ★ (owned) The book
  that made "urban legend" a household term, with readings of what each one means.
  *BND:* the genre vocabulary behind Folklore's "contemporary legend" label.
- [ ] **Linda Dégh, *Legend and Belief: Dialectics of a Folklore Genre*** (2001). ★ Legends as debates about belief:
  tellers and listeners argue over what's possible, and the argument is the genre.
  *BND:* why "people arguing over a claim" counts as folklore too (the circulation check on fact-checked rumors).
- [ ] **Bill Ellis, *Aliens, Ghosts, and Cults: Legends We Live*** (2001). Legends people act out (ostension), not
  just tell: the step from story to deed.
  *BND:* watch for motifs that turn into action in the news (a rumor followed by a real incident).
- [ ] **Gary Alan Fine, *Manufacturing Tales: Sex and Money in Contemporary Legends*** (1992). Legends about
  corporations, contamination and commerce, read as moral commentary on the economy.
  *BND:* a model for the economic motifs (price gouging, elite greed) in the index.
- [ ] **Patricia A. Turner, *I Heard It Through the Grapevine: Rumor in African-American Culture*** (1993). Rumors as
  a community stating a truth it can't prove: contamination, conspiracy and distrust with real roots.
  *BND:* reading a rumor's grievance seriously without endorsing its claim, the stance of the Folklore page.
- [ ] **Gary Alan Fine & Patricia A. Turner, *Whispers on the Color Line: Rumor and Race in America*** (2001). The
  same rumor shapes told on both sides of the racial divide, each casting the other as the threat.
  *BND:* directly about "one side says": motifs both sides tell with the roles swapped.
- [ ] **Gary Alan Fine & Bill Ellis, *The Global Grapevine: Why Rumors of Terrorism, Immigration, and Trade
  Matter*** (2010). ★ Rumors about terrorists, immigrants and foreign trade as the folklore of globalization and its
  politics.
  *BND:* the closest match to the thesis; "Immigration as infiltration" and "Handouts for newcomers" are its subject.
- [ ] **Nicholas DiFonzo & Prashant Bordia, *Rumor Psychology: Social and Organizational Approaches*** (2007). A
  modern synthesis: uncertainty, anxiety, trust and belief as the drivers of transmission.
  *BND:* variables to test against the corpus: does anxiety in the news predict which motifs spread?
- [ ] **Cass R. Sunstein, *On Rumors*** (2009). A short account of cascades and group polarization: how rumors spread
  and why correcting them can backfire.
  *BND:* why fact-checks sit beside a rumor on the site rather than replacing it.

## B. Classifying stories: indexes, types and structure

How folklorists have catalogued recurring shapes, the tradition the BND Motif Index joins, and the arguments about
how to do it.

- [ ] **Stith Thompson, *Motif-Index of Folk-Literature*** (revised edition, 6 vols., 1955–58). The great catalogue of
  motifs, lettered A to Z by subject. Browse it rather than read it.
  *BND:* the model for a motif index, and the lesson from its critics: a top-down scheme fits poorly (why ours grows
  bottom-up).
- [ ] **Hans-Jörg Uther, *The Types of International Folktales* (ATU)** (2004). The tale-type index revised: whole
  plots rather than single motifs, with cross-references.
  *BND:* the difference between a motif and a type: a saga or narrative thread is closer to a type.
- [ ] **Stith Thompson, *The Folktale*** (1946). A readable survey that explains what a motif is and how the indexes
  were meant to be used.
  *BND:* the best plain definition of "motif" to borrow for the methods page.
- [ ] **Vladimir Propp, *Morphology of the Folktale*** (1928; English 1958). Tales broken into a fixed sequence of
  functions performed by a small cast of roles: villain, hero, helper.
  *BND:* the ancestor of the story's cast (🦹 villain, 🫂 victim, 🦸 hero) on Folklore cards.
- [ ] **Alan Dundes (ed.), *The Blood Libel Legend: A Casebook in Anti-Semitic Folklore*** (1991). ★ Essays
  following one deadly accusation from medieval Europe into the modern era.
  *BND:* the case study for "campaigns reuse shapes": its modern descendants are in today's rumors.
- [ ] **Alan Dundes, *Interpreting Folklore*** (1980). Essays on reading folklore for what it says about the group
  that tells it, including "Who Are the Folk?".
  *BND:* answers whether partisan online publics count as "folk" (they do, by Dundes's definition).
- [ ] **Barre Toelken, *The Dynamics of Folklore*** (1979). A textbook on how folklore varies while staying
  recognizably the same.
  *BND:* the theory behind the copied ↔ own-words meter: variation is the sign of living tradition.

## C. Conspiracy and moral panic

The political genres built from legend shapes: hidden enemies, secret plots and folk devils.

- [ ] **Richard Hofstadter, *The Paranoid Style in American Politics*** (1964 essay; 1965 book). The essay that named
  the recurring American style of seeing politics as a vast conspiracy.
  *BND:* a historical baseline: which "paranoid style" motifs recur in 2026?
- [ ] **Michael Barkun, *A Culture of Conspiracy: Apocalyptic Visions in Contemporary America*** (2003; 2nd ed.
  2013). ★ Event, systemic and superconspiracies, and how fringe ideas merge into one "improvisational" worldview.
  *BND:* the source of the conspiracy scopes the site labels in previews.
- [ ] **Kathryn S. Olmsted, *Real Enemies: Conspiracy Theories and American Democracy, World War I to 9/11***
  (2009). How real government secrecy fed conspiracy theories about the government.
  *BND:* why "government deception" motifs aren't always baseless: a check on how the index is read.
- [ ] **Joseph E. Uscinski & Joseph M. Parent, *American Conspiracy Theories*** (2014). A century of letters to the
  editor coded for conspiracy talk: who blames whom, and when it rises.
  *BND:* a model of long-run coding over a corpus, like what the archive could support.
- [ ] **Jesse Walker, *The United States of Paranoia: A Conspiracy Theory*** (2013). American conspiracy stories
  sorted into five recurring enemies: outside, within, above, below and benevolent.
  *BND:* five ready-made candidates for top-level motif kinds, to compare against what grows bottom-up.
- [ ] **Norman Cohn, *Warrant for Genocide: The Myth of the Jewish World-Conspiracy and the Protocols of the Elders of
  Zion*** (1967). How a forgery became the template for "secret cabal" conspiracies.
  *BND:* the ancestor of "Elite cabal": know it to spot it under new names.
- [ ] **Stanley Cohen, *Folk Devils and Moral Panics*** (1972). How media and authorities turn a group into a threat
  to society's values.
  *BND:* the frame for questions like "what's the shape of the data-center panic?"
- [ ] **Jeffrey S. Victor, *Satanic Panic: The Creation of a Contemporary Legend*** (1993). How a legend about hidden
  ritual abuse became a national panic, step by step.
  *BND:* a legend's full life cycle into policy: a template for following a thread across months.
- [ ] **Russell Muirhead & Nancy L. Rosenblum, *A Lot of People Are Saying: The New Conspiracism and the Assault on
  Democracy*** (2019). "Conspiracy without the theory": assertions passed on by repetition, with no explanation
  needed.
  *BND:* why some claims are bare assertions with no story; a reason to allow "no motif".
- [ ] **Anna Merlan, *Republic of Lies: American Conspiracy Theorists and Their Surprising Rise to Power*** (2019).
  Reported fieldwork among conspiracy communities on the way to mainstream politics.
  *BND:* context for the fringe-to-front-page path the site can measure.

## D. Propaganda and information warfare

The deliberate side: how states, parties and networks use the stories people already tell.

- [ ] **Jacques Ellul, *Propaganda: The Formation of Men's Attitudes*** (1962; English 1965). Propaganda works
  through existing myths and needs rather than against them; it's demanded as much as imposed.
  *BND:* the theoretical core of the thesis, from the propaganda side.
- [ ] **Thomas Rid, *Active Measures: The Secret History of Disinformation and Political Warfare*** (2020). ★ A
  century of state disinformation, from the KGB to the internet, built on real fears and real documents.
  *BND:* case material for flagging campaign-linked claims and seeing which motifs they ride.
- [ ] **Yochai Benkler, Robert Faris & Hal Roberts, *Network Propaganda: Manipulation, Disinformation, and
  Radicalization in American Politics*** (2018). Millions of stories and links mapped: an asymmetric media ecosystem,
  measured.
  *BND:* method kin to the site's lean, blindspot and wire-share measures.
- [ ] **Peter Pomerantsev, *This Is Not Propaganda: Adventures in the War Against Reality*** (2019). Reporting on how
  modern influence work dissolves shared facts rather than selling one story.
  *BND:* the "firehose" motifs: when the shape is confusion itself.
- [ ] **Renée DiResta, *Invisible Rulers: The People Who Turn Lies into Reality*** (2024). Influencers, algorithms and
  crowds as the new propaganda machine, from a researcher who studied it from inside.
  *BND:* who carries a motif from posts to front pages: the path the folklore and news pages trace.
- [ ] **Francesca Bolla Tripodi, *The Propagandists' Playbook: How Conservative Elites Manipulate Search and Threaten
  Democracy*** (2022). How familiar interpretive habits ("do your own research", close reading of texts) are used to
  steer belief.
  *BND:* explains the "Caught on camera" and scrutinize-the-video motifs.
- [ ] **Samuel C. Woolley & Philip N. Howard (eds.), *Computational Propaganda: Political Parties, Politicians, and
  Political Manipulation on Social Media*** (2018). Country studies of bots and coordinated campaigns.
  *BND:* background for telling copypasta (coordinated) from folklore (retold), as the copied meter does.

## E. Narrative and political myth

How politics runs on stories: deep stories, myths and spectacles.

- [ ] **Arlie Russell Hochschild, *Strangers in Their Own Land: Anger and Mourning on the American Right*** (2016).
  ★ The "deep story": the narrative that feels true beneath the facts, here of waiting in line while others cut ahead.
  *BND:* a deep story is what a general motif names; "Handouts for newcomers" is her line-cutting story.
- [ ] **George Lakoff, *Moral Politics: How Liberals and Conservatives Think*** (1996). Two family models (strict
  father, nurturant parent) under the two sides' moral language.
  *BND:* a hypothesis to test: do the sides' motifs split along these frames?
- [ ] **Francesca Polletta, *It Was Like a Fever: Storytelling in Protest and Politics*** (2006). When stories
  persuade in politics and when they backfire, from sit-ins to policy debates.
  *BND:* why the Focus Group voters' stories matter as evidence, not anecdote.
- [ ] **Murray Edelman, *Constructing the Political Spectacle*** (1988). Problems, leaders and enemies as
  constructions the news presents as facts.
  *BND:* reading the front pages themselves as a genre, not a window.
- [ ] **Roland Barthes, *Mythologies*** (1957). Short essays decoding the myths in everyday images and news: how a
  sign comes to feel natural.
  *BND:* a model for the short readings a motif page could give each shape.
- [ ] **Ernst Cassirer, *The Myth of the State*** (1946). A philosopher's account of how myth returned to politics in
  the 1930s, written as he died.
  *BND:* the long view: political myth as a recurring condition, not a modern glitch.

## F. Digital and computational folkloristics

Folklore online, and reading it at scale: the methods side of what BND builds.

- [ ] **Timothy R. Tangherlini and colleagues, "An automated pipeline for the discovery of conspiracy and conspiracy
  theory narrative frameworks: Bridgegate, Pizzagate and storytelling on the web"** (2020, article, *PLOS ONE*). ★
  Posts read at scale for actors and their relations, showing a conspiracy framework stabilizing from scattered
  threads.
  *BND:* the nearest research to the motif index and the cast; a model for narrative frameworks next.
- [ ] **Russell Frank, *Newslore: Contemporary Folklore on the Internet*** (2011). ★ (owned) The jokes, doctored
  photos and rumors people make in response to news events, from 9/11 to elections and disasters: folklore as a running
  commentary on the news.
  *BND:* the exact meeting point of the site's two halves: what people retell (Folklore) set beside what the front
  pages report (the "In the news" link on each card).
- [ ] **Whitney Phillips & Ryan M. Milner, *You Are Here: A Field Guide for Navigating Polarized Speech, Conspiracy
  Theories, and Our Polluted Media Landscape*** (2021). A folklore-literate account of how deep memetic frames spread
  through networks.
  *BND:* their "deep memetic frames" are close kin to general motifs.
- [ ] **Whitney Phillips & Ryan M. Milner, *The Ambivalent Internet: Mischief, Oddity, and Antagonism Online***
  (2017). Online folklore as both play and harm, often at once.
  *BND:* a check on reading jokes and satire as straight claims.
- [ ] **Limor Shifman, *Memes in Digital Culture*** (2014). A short, clear definition of internet memes as groups of
  related texts made with awareness of each other.
  *BND:* her definition fits the narrative finder's groups of retold posts.
- [ ] **Trevor J. Blank (ed.), *Folk Culture in the Digital Age: The Emergent Dynamics of Human Interaction***
  (2012). Folklorists on how tradition works online.
  *BND:* vocabulary for describing online retelling to folklorists.
- [ ] **Franco Moretti, *Distant Reading*** (2013). Essays arguing for reading literature at scale: patterns no close
  reader could see.
  *BND:* the case for the archive as a corpus, paired with the board's close reading.
